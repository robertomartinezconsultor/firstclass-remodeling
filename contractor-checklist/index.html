Is your contractor
legit?

10 things every San Antonio homeowner should verify before signing. Check each one. Share the results.

Written contract with detailed scope
Not a handshake. Not a text message. A written document listing every item: materials, timeline, payment schedule, change order process. Why it matters: Texas RCLA law requires written notice for defect claims. No contract = no legal protection.
General liability insurance (certificate provided)
Ask for a Certificate of Insurance naming you as certificate holder. San Antonio requires $300K-$500K minimum per occurrence. Why it matters: if a worker is injured on your property without insurance, you could be liable.
Workers compensation coverage
Separate from general liability. Covers workers injured on the job. Without it, your homeowner's insurance may have to cover medical bills. Why it matters: TX does not require workers comp, but reputable firms carry it voluntarily.
City registration (if pulling permits)
San Antonio requires contractors to register with the Development Services Department before pulling permits. Ask for the registration number. Why it matters: unpermitted work can void your insurance and reduce your home's resale value.
Verifiable references with photos
Not just names. Ask for photos of completed projects in your area. Better: ask to visit a finished project. Best: talk to the homeowner directly. Why it matters: photos prove capability. A conversation reveals reliability.
Payment schedule tied to milestones
Never pay 100% upfront. Standard: deposit (10-25%), progress payments at milestones, final payment on walkthrough approval. Why it matters: staggered payments keep the contractor motivated to finish strong — especially the details.
Lien waiver provided with each payment
A lien waiver confirms that subcontractors and suppliers have been paid. Without it, a supplier could place a lien on your home even if you paid the contractor. Why it matters: in Texas, mechanics liens can be filed up to 4 months after work is complete.
Timeline in writing with start and end dates
A vague "4-6 weeks" is not a timeline. Get specific start date, milestone dates, and expected completion date. In writing. Why it matters: without dates, there is no accountability. "Soon" is not a schedule.
Change order process defined
Mid-project changes happen. The contract should say: changes are in writing, signed by both parties, with adjusted cost and timeline before work proceeds. Why it matters: verbal changes are the #1 source of invoice disputes in remodeling.
Warranty on workmanship (1 year minimum)
Materials have manufacturer warranties. But workmanship should be warranted separately by the contractor. Ask for it in writing. Why it matters: a tile that cracks because of bad substrate prep is not a material defect — it is workmanship.
